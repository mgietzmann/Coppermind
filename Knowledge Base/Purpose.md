To write a discussion or an introduction you need context. Context means reading a whole bunch of research and forming an overall picture of a space. Two problems immediately come out of this:
- How do you keep the space up to date as new research arises?
- How do you prevent yourself from re-reading the same papers every time the space you're looking at changes?
Well what if you built a wiki? Specifically, what if each time you read a paper you tried to add its context to a wiki. You'd get compression thanks to the fact that you wouldn't need to repeat things repeated in other papers (you can just link to the new paper as well) and you'd also get compression for super specific details that you'd never reference anyways. Therefore the wiki will compress your reading down like crazy and mean you can quickly form new contexts for new spaces without having to do any re-reading. 

You'll still need to gather up papers for each new space to capture things you haven't read, but it should prevent re-reading things you have. And then you'll have a lovely format to look things up in when you eventually go to write up your introduction or discussion sections. 

I'm going to give this a try for my current discussion. We'll start by reading one paper and seeding my wiki that way. 'Cause I know I'm going to need a process for creating wikis in the first place. 

## When do You Have the Context?

When there are no more questions! At least ones with answers you can find from others. 

As such it's rather important you keep track of your questions as you go along. Both ones relevant to your research question and those that could be cool pursuits for future study. 

There's definitely a cool algorithm hinted at here for gaining context (or at least gaining paths to context) 

## Use Cases

1. **Taking the Whole Mine**: Every paper is filled with so many goodies. If you only pay attention to the stuff relevant to your paper you'll miss out on so much along the way. If you do take it all but don't organize it you'll never find it again. Take the whole mine but recognize the need to organize it. 
2. **The Atom is the Idea/Fact**: A single idea or fact can be related to a lot of things. A paper as a whole may not be related to all of those things. Filing a paper on Chinook salmon bycatch under whales because there was a mention of whale bycatch is just going to create a lot of noise. 
3. **Pull What You Find**: The ultimate goal is that after doing a whole bunch of mining you can take an area/topic/etc. and pull in all the gems you discovered about it. This means while the atoms are there for organizational reasons, they need to be pulled together after the fact. 
4. **Hierarchies Remain**: I might be interested in just Chinook bycatch, or I might be interested in bycatch in general. Hierarchy can make this work rather quickly and effectively and hierarchy can be assigned after the fact when we pull tags together into higher categories. 
5. **Metadata is Necessary if not Sufficient**: It's all great to have notes, but they're slightly useless if you don't remember where they come from. Dates and order can also be helpful too for summaries. Hell a priority order might some day be useful. So having meta data is also essential. 

So here's the idea. Take each note and store it under a series of lowest level tags - Chinook salmon depth behavior for example. Then create a series of associated tags Chinook salmon, depth behavior. And then allow for summarization of all of these. 

And notes can be created as a document themselves that can be ingested by this kind of tool. Makes everything super simple! 

So maybe the thing to focus on is the ingest form.
